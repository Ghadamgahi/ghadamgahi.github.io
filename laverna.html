Overview

LAVERNA is a fictional four-member music group whose entire output — music, video, artwork and identity — is generated, edited and published by one person. It exists as a working testbed for a question more interesting than one-off image generation: what does it take to run generative AI as a production pipeline rather than a toy?

Single images are easy. Serialised content is not. The moment the same four faces need to appear across multiple videos, wardrobes, locations and lighting setups — and still be recognisable at thumbnail size on a phone — the problem stops being prompting and becomes design system work.

Real numbers give the volume: one recent billing cycle covered 5,892 generations — 5,080 images, 792 video clips, 7,061 seconds of rendered footage. A single three-minute music video in this pipeline is roughly twenty overlapping ten-second segments, cross-cutting two locations with nineteen dancers in exact unison across every cut, lip-synced to the audio at frame accuracy. Every generation runs against the same rig; nothing is rebuilt bespoke.

What follows are the five problems that actually took engineering, and how each was solved.

01 · Character consistency

The central technical constraint. A group only works if viewers recognise its members, which means the same face has to survive thousands of generations across three distinct wardrobe eras per member — street, formal, and finale — plus multiple hair and styling variants within each.

Reference conditioning solves this, but only when the reference itself is built correctly. The first character sheets used wide shots with low facial detail — and every generation drawing on them produced near-miss faces that broke recognition immediately.

A face reference should be flat-lit, frontal and neutral — the opposite of the dramatic, high-contrast portraits it will later be used to produce. Shadow hides features, and hidden features get invented.

Rebuilding references as clean, evenly lit, frontal portraits with sharp eye detail fixed identity drift across the entire pipeline, for both image and video generation. The same rig now carries each character through every era without a new reference build.

02 · Prompt architecture

Writing a bespoke prompt per shot doesn't scale. Every prompt in the pipeline is a parameterised template: a fixed structural body with swappable variables for character token, wardrobe, lighting temperature, expression, camera position and aspect ratio.

That yields three things that matter in production:

  • Reproducibility — a look can be recreated months later rather than rediscovered by trial and error.
  • Controlled iteration — one variable changes at a time, so any failure is attributable.
  • Throughput — a single template covers four characters and every era, instead of sixteen bespoke prompts.

Prompts are also ordered by how models actually weight instruction. Subject and framing lead; background and lighting are stated explicitly rather than implied — an unstated background reliably defaults to a studio backdrop, which is rarely what a scene needs.

03 · Documented failure modes

Most of the value in a mature prompt system sits in its negatives. These are recurring failures hit in production and the constraints that resolved them — each found by diagnosis rather than by rerolling until something worked.

04 · Working around the platform

Long-form production against hosted models runs into refusals that have nothing to do with the actual content. Diagnosing them — rather than rerolling around them or abandoning the shot — was a repeated part of the pipeline.

Cascading classifier rejections

Content classifiers rarely fire in isolation. In practice, a single acoustic or visual signal triggering one classifier cascades into adjacent categories — an audio flag paired with a copyright flag, or an NSFW flag paired with a violence flag, on content that is unambiguously safe. Reading the pair as evidence of a cascade rather than as two independent claims makes the diagnosis tractable.

Audio as a rejection surface

Musical drops with distorted synths, pitched vocal chops, or dense high-frequency content routinely false-positive as "screams," "distress," or copyrighted audio. Instrumental sections that pass in isolation fail once fed to the video model as a lip-sync reference.

The working fix is a small Audacity chain: low-pass filter at ~2 kHz, pitch shift down an octave, and heavy reverb — enough to defeat the classifier's acoustic fingerprint while preserving the rhythm and dynamics the dancers need to hit. The original audio is layered back over the render in post.

Vocabulary that reads as neutral

Weapons and violence classifiers pattern-match on words as much as on images. "Mortar tube," "ignition," "burst," "beams cutting between towers," "sparks cascading" — all read as violence-adjacent to text classifiers regardless of context. Swapping to "launch canister," "trigger switch," "bloom," "beams crossing," and "trails drifting down" clears refusals with no change to the visual intent.

The underlying skill is treating the platform as part of the surface. Prompt design, audio preprocessing, and vocabulary discipline are the same problem in three different modalities: engineering around opaque classifiers that will not tell you why they said no.

05 · Creative measured, not guessed

Generative output is cheap, which makes taste the bottleneck — and taste is testable. Every creative decision in the pipeline is evaluated against live audience data rather than personal preference.

Thumbnails judged at delivery size

Thumbnails were failing not because they were poorly made, but because they were indistinguishable from their neighbours — the same bright, busy, multi-subject composition as every competing tile in the feed. Diagnosing that required rendering candidates at 168px, the size they actually appear at, and compositing them into real sidebar screenshots rather than judging them full-screen.

The rebuild inverted the variables that mattered: one large face instead of four small ones, near-black instead of bright, empty instead of busy. Contrast against neighbours — not intrinsic quality — is what wins the click.

Metadata as an audience-steering lever

Traffic-source analysis showed suggested placement dominated by a single major artist whose audience watched roughly a quarter of each video — high volume, poor fit. Adjusting title metadata moved placement into a broader, better-matched segment where equivalent traffic watched substantially more of the same content.

The lesson generalises: where creative is served often matters more than the creative itself — and metadata, not artwork, is the lever that moves it.

Format decisions from measured behaviour

Comparing formats on the same channel, narrative content sustained an average view duration of 1:25 against 0:51 for music-video content — roughly 67% more watch time per viewer — on fewer total views, with a 95–100% positive like ratio across releases.

That reframed the production roadmap: the format that looked secondary was in fact carrying more attention per viewer than the flagship one. Deciding what to build next became a data question rather than a taste question.

Visual identity as a system

Consistency across a property is a design system problem, not an art problem. LAVERNA runs on a defined and documented set of rules:

  • Typography — a single display face with fixed logo-block proportions (20% of frame width, 7.5% of height) that scale cleanly across every format.
  • Colour — each member owns a colour, locked to the person rather than her current hair, so creative eras can change without the system breaking.
  • Composition grammar — a shared thumbnail language (low camera, lifted chin, neutral expression) that makes unrelated videos read as one channel.
  • Format discipline — every asset designed for both 16:9 and 9:16 delivery and validated at mobile scale first, since the majority of views are phone views.

What transfers

The project is a music group, but the work underneath it is the work these systems need in any commercial setting: maintaining brand and character consistency at volume, building prompt systems other people could run, diagnosing model and platform failure instead of rerolling past it, and closing the loop between generated creative and measured performance.

The medium was chosen because it is unforgiving. Serialised characters break loudly when consistency fails, and a public channel gives honest feedback every single day.
